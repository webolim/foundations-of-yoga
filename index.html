Answer: The Pancha Koshas, or five sheaths, are the five layers of the human being, as described in the Taittiriya Upanishad. They represent the different levels of our existence, from the gross physical body to the subtle spiritual self. The five koshas are:

  1. Annamaya Kosha (The Food Sheath): This is the physical body, made of and sustained by food. It is the grossest and most tangible of the five sheaths.
  2. Pranamaya Kosha (The Vital Air Sheath): This sheath is composed of prana, the vital life force energy that animates the body and mind. It is responsible for all physiological functions, such as breathing, circulation, and digestion.
  3. Manomaya Kosha (The Mental Sheath): This is the sheath of the mind, which includes our thoughts, emotions, and feelings. It is the level of our consciousness that processes sensory information and reacts to the external world.
  4. Vijnanamaya Kosha (The Wisdom Sheath): This sheath is the level of our higher intellect, intuition, and wisdom. It is the faculty of discrimination that allows us to understand and make judgments.
  5. Anandamaya Kosha (The Bliss Sheath): This is the innermost and most subtle of the five sheaths. It is the causal body and the source of our deepest joy and bliss. It is the level of our being that is closest to the Atman, or the true Self.

The six orthodox schools of Indian philosophy are known as Astika Darshanas. They accept the authority of the Vedas as the supreme revealed scriptures. These schools are:

  • Samkhya: A dualistic philosophy that posits a clear distinction between Purusha (consciousness) and Prakriti (matter).
  • Yoga: A school that accepts the Samkhya philosophy but also includes the concept of Ishvara (God) and a practical path to liberation.
  • Nyaya: A school of logic and epistemology that focuses on the means of acquiring valid knowledge.
  • Vaisheshika: An atomistic school that describes the world in terms of categories or padarthas.
  • Mimamsa: A school that focuses on the exegesis of the Vedas, particularly the Samhita and Brahmana portions.
  • Vedanta: A school that focuses on the philosophical teachings of the Upanishads.

The three heterodox schools of Indian philosophy are known as Nastika Darshanas. They do not accept the authority of the Vedas. These schools are:

  • Charvaka: A materialistic school that rejects the existence of a soul, God, and an afterlife.
  • Buddhism: A philosophy and religion founded by Siddhartha Gautama that teaches the path to enlightenment and liberation from suffering.
  • Jainism: A religion and philosophy that emphasizes non-violence, self-control, and asceticism as the path to liberation.

  9. to 30. The five Mahabhutas (gross elements): Ether, air, fire, water, and earth.

Samkhya Theory of Causation (Satkaryavada):

The Samkhya school of philosophy advocates the theory of Satkaryavada, which states that the effect (karya) pre-exists in its material cause (karana). This means that the effect is not a new creation but a manifestation of what was already latent in the cause. For example, a pot (effect) pre-exists in the clay (cause) in an unmanifest form. The potter does not create the pot out of nothing, but simply gives a new form to the existing clay. This theory is based on the idea that something cannot come out of nothing (ex nihilo nihil fit).

The three main schools of Vedanta philosophy are:

  1. Advaita Vedanta (Non-dualism): Propounded by Adi Shankaracharya, this school holds that the ultimate reality (Brahman) is one and non-dual. The individual self (Atman) is identical with Brahman, and the perceived world of multiplicity is an illusion (maya).
  2. Vishishtadvaita Vedanta (Qualified Non-dualism): Propounded by Ramanujacharya, this school holds that Brahman is the supreme reality, but the individual souls and the material world are also real and are eternally related to Brahman as its attributes or modes.
  3. Dvaita Vedanta (Dualism): Propounded by Madhvacharya, this school holds that there is a fundamental and eternal distinction between Brahman (God), the individual souls, and the material world.

The Three Levels of Sathya (Truth or Reality) in Advaita Vedanta:

  1. Paramarthika Satya (Absolute Reality): This is the ultimate, unchanging reality of Brahman, which is non-dual and beyond all attributes.
  2. Vyavaharika Satya (Empirical Reality): This is the reality of the everyday world of our experience, which is considered to be real from a conventional point of view but is ultimately illusory (maya).
  3. Pratibhasika Satya (Apparent Reality): This is the reality of dreams and illusions, which is considered to be real only for the duration of the experience.

Differences: The main difference between Samkhya and Yoga is that Yoga accepts the concept of Ishvara (God), while Samkhya does not. Yoga is also a practical philosophy that provides a path to liberation, while Samkhya is a purely theoretical philosophy.

Nyaya and Vaisheshika:

Similarities: Both Nyaya and Vaisheshika are realistic philosophies that believe in the existence of an external world. They also share a similar atomistic theory of the universe.

Differences: The main difference between Nyaya and Vaisheshika is that Nyaya focuses on logic and epistemology, while Vaisheshika focuses on metaphysics and ontology. Nyaya outlines four pramanas (means of knowledge), while Vaisheshika identifies seven categories of reality.

Mimamsa and Vedanta:

Similarities: Both Mimamsa and Vedanta are based on the authority of the Vedas. They also share a similar belief in the importance of dharma (righteousness) and karma (action).

Differences: The main difference between Mimamsa and Vedanta is that Mimamsa focuses on the ritualistic aspects of the Vedas, while Vedanta focuses on the philosophical aspects. Mimamsa is concerned with the correct performance of rituals, while Vedanta is concerned with the nature of reality and the path to liberation.

Karma Yoga: The Path of Action

Karma Yoga is the path of selfless action. It is the practice of performing one's duties with dedication and skill, without being attached to the fruits of the actions. The Karma Yogi works for the welfare of the world, without any desire for personal gain or recognition. The goal of Karma Yoga is to purify the mind and to become free from the cycle of karma.

Jnana Yoga: The Path of Knowledge

Jnana Yoga is the path of knowledge and wisdom. It is the process of inquiry into the nature of reality and the self. The Jnana Yogi seeks to understand the true nature of the Atman (the individual self) and its identity with Brahman (the ultimate reality). The path of Jnana Yoga involves the practices of shravana (listening), manana (reflecting), and nididhyasana (meditating).

Vedanta Darshan: The Culmination of the Vedas

Vedanta Darshan is one of the six orthodox schools of Indian philosophy and is based on the philosophical teachings of the Upanishads. The central theme of Vedanta is the nature of Brahman, the ultimate reality, and the means to realize it. The three main schools of Vedanta are Advaita Vedanta, Vishishtadvaita Vedanta, and Dvaita Vedanta.

Samkhya Darshan: The Philosophy of Dualism

Samkhya Darshan is a dualistic philosophy that posits a clear distinction between Purusha (consciousness) and Prakriti (matter). It is a purely theoretical philosophy that provides a metaphysical framework for understanding the nature of reality.
